analyze/funnel.py · open PR

One blended number hides where the money leaks

Counting stage transitions throws away two things that matter: direction and value. A deal moving toward closed-won and a deal moving toward closed-lost both count as exactly one. Giving every stage its own dollar outcome closes that gap — and immediately turns up a finding a single ROI number cannot show.

The blind spot this closes

The count outcome sums the transition tensor over both stage axes. Direction and value fall out in that sum. So a channel can look excellent at moving deals while being the reason they die later — and nothing in a blended number would tell you.

The ladder

Four stages, top to bottom. Two of the CRM's stages deliberately aren't rungs.

What a stage level means

Two rules define it, and together they buy a free correctness check.

Why the invariant is worth having

Because credit is backfilled, stage dollars are cumulative downward — the series can only fall as you go down the funnel, so every stage-to-stage ratio lands in [0, 1] and is a genuine leak rate. That's not just tidy: a ratio above 1 is impossible, so the check doubles as a probe on the data-prep layer. If one ever appears, prep stopped crediting skipped stages.

Dollars driven to each stage

Per channel, per stage. Recovered as expected ROI × actual spend — the channel-off counterfactual, multiplied back.

ROI at every stage

The same ROI contract as the main egress, repeated once per stage.

Read across a row, not down a column

Every channel returns less as you go down — that's just the funnel narrowing. The question worth asking is whether the order of the channels holds from top to bottom. Here it doesn't.

REVERSAL

LinkedIn Ads wins the top of the funnel and loses the bottom

It generates leads more cheaply than anything else, then leaks harder than Search at every step down. On revenue that actually closed, Search overtakes it. A single blended ROI averages this away completely.

Ranked on marginal ROI — the payoff of the next dollar — because that's what a reallocation decision actually turns on.

Rank reversals

Channels whose rank at the top of the funnel differs from the bottom.

Why credibility is a between-channel question

A reversal means channel X outranked Y at the top and Y outranked X at the bottom. What has to be believable is the X-against-Y gap at each end, so credible is true only when the pair separates at both. Comparing one channel's own interval across stages would answer a different question — and answer it trivially, since stage dollars fall so hard that a channel's own intervals almost never overlap across stages.

Carry-through — why the reversal happens

The fraction of each stage's dollars that survive to the next.

The pair naming isn't cosmetic

Columns are named <to>/<from>. On a partial funnel the neighbouring stage isn't the adjacent one, and a bare closed_won column would quietly read as opportunity-to-won when it's really lead-to-won.

The core idea · step 1 of 6

Turn it off and see what breaks

A fitted model does not contain ROI. It contains decay rates, saturation constants, and per-account effects — not one number a marketer would ask for. Every KPI has to be built, and the move is always the same.

1

Replay the world as it happened

Push the real spend back through the fitted model. It reproduces roughly what actually occurred, because that's what it was fit to.

2

Replay it again with one channel switched off

Same model, same everything, except LinkedIn Ads spend is set to zero for the whole year. Some revenue survives — the customers who'd have come anyway.

3

The gap is what the channel earned

Difference the two worlds, divide by what was spent. That's the return multiple — and it's a causal question, not a share of credit.

Why not just count clicks

Counting the sales that touched an ad takes credit for the weather. Some of those people were going to buy regardless. The only honest question is how much outcome disappears when the channel does.

All four thousand versions, the whole way through

The single most important implementation decision.

The model doesn't produce one answer, it produces four thousand — each a coherent version of how the year could have worked. Every step above runs on all four thousand, and they collapse to a median and a 90% range only at the very last moment.

Why it can't be done the cheap way

Adstock and saturation are curved, so the average of the answers isn't the answer for the average. Collapse early and you get a biased number with a confident-looking error bar bolted onto it.

Median, not mean

Deal sizes are skewed about 44× between typical and largest. The mean gets dragged around by a handful of enormous deals; the median describes an account you'd actually recognise.

What optimising actually does

It moves budget away from whichever channel has the lowest marginal ROI — the value of its next dollar — toward whichever has the highest, repeating until every channel's next dollar is worth about the same. That equal-marginal-return point is the mathematical optimum. Note it is not the channel with the best average ROI that wins the money; it's the one whose curve still has room to climb. Watch the spread above collapse toward zero as it converges.

How this differs from the real solver

This page uses equal-marginal-return hill climbing, which is the intuition behind the answer. The production path calls scipy.optimize.minimize with SLSQP (maxiter 200, ftol 1e-7) against the full posterior — it maximises mean predicted outcome across all 4,000 draws subject to shares summing to 1 and per-channel bounds, then projects back into those bounds. Same optimality condition, but run once per draw rather than on a single illustrative curve.

Why the denominator carries both σ

This is a two-sample Z. Treating the experiment as exact ground truth with zero error would manufacture CONFLICTs out of noisy lift tests. Widen either σ and the same gap becomes less damning — which is correct, because two uncertain estimates disagreeing by a little is not evidence of anything.

Note the flip side, which the source calls intended: a very tight posterior trips CONFLICT on a small gap, while an under-converged one can barely reach TENSION even on a large gap. That's why convergence is gate 1 — an under-converged fit should be stopped before it ever gets here.

The model is never re-fitted

This is a fit-once, read-only sensitivity analysis. Re-running the sampler with a different seed would only measure Monte Carlo noise — the RFC explicitly rules that out ("Pillar 3 is NOT random-seed resampling"). A loop also only fails on a beyond-noise reorder: if two channels' 90% intervals overlap, or their medians sit within 5%, swapping them is a tie, not instability.

Final verdict
—
Why the order matters

These aren't three scores to average — they're a sequence, and each one is only meaningful if the previous passed. Fail convergence and the numbers aren't numbers. Pass convergence but conflict with a lift test and the model is converged and wrong. Pass both but fail stability and the model may well be right, yet the specific recommendation isn't robust enough to move budget on. A model can be stable and wrong, or right and unstable, which is exactly why one gate can't replace the others.

Why a count can't be compared to a dollar

Qualified Leads and Stage Transitions have no dollar value attached, so their "ROI" is a rate — advances per dollar. A 0.004 next to a 4.55× isn't a worse channel, it's a different unit. That's also why count outcomes additionally emit cost_per_outcome_usd, which inverts to dollars-per-thing and is the number a human can actually reason about.

Shared inputs

Adstock happens once, upstream of all four. It describes how this channel's exposure lingers, not how any one outcome responds to it — so the same adstocked exposure feeds every card below. Only saturation and the coefficient after that point are per-outcome.

These four curves are illustrative

Coefficient, half-saturation and slope below are hand-picked so the four outcomes look sensibly different from each other. They are not derived from a fit. In the real system every one of them comes out of NUTS sampling against actual history, with a different value on every posterior draw. The shapes and the refusal logic are real; the specific numbers are not.

The refusal is the point

Drag a signal-strength slider low enough and that outcome stops answering — exactly what Pipeline Revenue does in the live scenario. The ESS gate is checked before anything is computed, so a thin outcome never produces a confident-looking number that happens to rest on nothing. An honest "not enough data here" is more useful than a precise guess, and it's the behaviour that makes the other three trustworthy.

Why four chains

That is the convergence check. Four independent explorations start from different random points; if they all describe the same landscape, the sampler found something real. A single run can't detect that failure.

Three-level pooling

Global channel effect → segment offset → account offset.

With 20 accounts you can't fit each independently — you'd be fitting noise. Pooling pulls a thin-data account toward its segment's behaviour, while an account with plenty of data is allowed to depart from it. The model decides how hard to pull, based on how much accounts actually differ.

Why target accept is turned up

The hierarchy creates funnel geometry — a narrow neck the sampler can't step through cleanly. Two fixes: a non-centered reparameterization that flattens the funnel, and smaller steps. A handful of divergences at 0.8, some at 0.95, clear at 0.99 — for about 1.5–2× the runtime.
